DIY Drain Cleaning: What Actually Works and What Makes It Worse

Not every home remedy is harmless, especially on Everett's older cast-iron and clay drain lines.

By the Everett Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-19

A slow drain is one of the most common household plumbing complaints, and it's also one of the most common targets for DIY fixes, some of which genuinely help and some of which make the underlying problem worse or harder to diagnose later. Here's an honest breakdown of what actually works.

What Works: A Plunger, Used Correctly

For a single clogged fixture, a proper flange plunger, not the flat cup-style plunger meant for toilets, creates a real seal and enough pressure to clear a simple blockage from hair or soap buildup. This is genuinely effective and carries no risk to your pipes.

What Works: A Drain Snake for Reachable Clogs

A basic hand-crank drain snake can clear a clog within a few feet of the drain opening, which covers a large share of simple bathroom and kitchen sink clogs. This is a legitimate DIY tool when used gently, without forcing it against resistance, which can damage a joint or fitting in an older line.

What Doesn't Work Long-Term: Chemical Drain Cleaners

Caustic chemical drain cleaners can clear a clog temporarily, but they carry real downsides on older plumbing. The chemical reaction generates heat that can damage older PVC or stress already-corroded metal pipe, and if the clog doesn't fully clear, the caustic liquid sits in the pipe, creating a hazard for anyone attempting a subsequent plunger or snake repair. They also don't address root intrusion or a structural pipe issue at all, just temporarily displace it.

What Makes Things Worse: Repeated Snaking Without Diagnosis

If the same drain keeps clogging every few weeks despite repeated snaking, that pattern itself is diagnostic information being ignored. A drain that returns to the same problem repeatedly usually has a structural cause, root intrusion or a pipe defect, that snaking alone will never permanently fix. Continuing to snake it every month treats the symptom indefinitely instead of solving the actual problem once.

When to Call a Professional Instead

If a plunger and gentle hand-snaking don't clear the clog, if multiple drains are affected at once, or if the same single drain keeps clogging repeatedly, it's time for a proper diagnosis. See our drain cleaning page for how we diagnose recurring clogs with a camera inspection, and our hydro jetting page for what actually clears root intrusion and heavy buildup that basic tools can't reach.

The Bottom Line

A plunger and a basic hand snake are legitimate first steps for a simple, isolated clog. Chemical cleaners and repeated snaking without diagnosis both risk making a real underlying problem harder and more expensive to fix once you finally call for help.

A Note on Enzyme Drain Treatments

Enzyme-based drain treatments, marketed as a gentler alternative to caustic chemical cleaners, work by biologically breaking down organic buildup like hair and grease over time. They're genuinely safer for pipes than caustic chemicals, but they work slowly and won't clear an active, complete blockage. They're better suited as ongoing maintenance for a slow-draining line than as a fix for a drain that's already stopped completely.

What About Boiling Water?

Boiling water poured down a drain can help dissolve grease buildup in metal pipes, but it carries real risk on PVC pipe, which can soften or warp under sustained high heat, and on any joint sealed with certain older compounds. If you're not certain what your drain lines are made of, this isn't a risk-free trick, despite how often it gets recommended online.

Kitchen Drains Deserve Special Caution

Kitchen sink clogs are usually grease-related, and grease responds poorly to most home remedies. Hot water alone often just pushes softened grease further down the line where it re-hardens in a colder section of pipe, sometimes making the eventual clog harder to reach with a basic snake. A better habit is prevention: wiping grease into the trash rather than rinsing it down the drain, which avoids the problem developing in the first place.

If a kitchen drain is already slow despite good habits, that often points to buildup from before those habits started, which is exactly the kind of accumulated buildup that hydro jetting clears more thoroughly than any home remedy.

A Reasonable DIY Sequence

For a single slow drain with no history of recurrence, try a flange plunger first, then a hand snake if the plunger doesn't fully clear it. If neither works, or if the clog returns within a few weeks, stop there rather than escalating to chemicals, and call for a proper diagnosis instead. Two attempts is a reasonable limit before escalating; more than that risks pushing a simple problem into a more expensive one, since forcing a snake against real resistance can damage an aging joint that was otherwise stable. A gentle touch with any hand tool is always the safer default on a line you haven't inspected. When in doubt, stop and call rather than push harder. A stubborn clog is rarely worth risking damage to an aging line just to avoid a service call, and a repeat visit to fix new damage costs far more than the original clog would have.

Quick Answers

Is it ever okay to use a chemical cleaner? Sparingly, on a fresh, minor clog in modern PVC, but never on a recurring clog or older metal pipe, where the heat and caustic reaction pose a real risk of accelerating existing corrosion.

Can a plunger damage older pipes? No, a standard plunger's pressure is gentle enough for any pipe material.

How do I know if a clog is a main line issue? Multiple fixtures backing up at once, especially the lowest drains in the house, points to the main line, not one fixture.
