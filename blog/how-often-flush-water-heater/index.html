How Often Should You Flush Your Water Heater (and Does It Matter)?

Skipping this one maintenance step is one of the most common reasons a water heater fails years earlier than it should.

By the Everett Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-19

Flushing a tank water heater means draining it fully to remove sediment that's settled at the bottom over time. It's one of the simplest maintenance tasks in residential plumbing, and it's also one of the most commonly skipped, which is a real reason we see tanks fail years before their expected 10-12 year service life.

Where the Sediment Comes From

Municipal water carries dissolved minerals, calcium and magnesium primarily, that settle out as sediment inside a hot tank over time. Homes with older galvanized supply lines see this accelerated further, since corrosion particles from the aging pipe add to the mineral buildup already accumulating from the water itself.

What Sediment Buildup Actually Does

A layer of sediment at the tank bottom acts as insulation between the burner or heating element and the water above it, which reduces heating efficiency and increases energy cost. Worse, that insulating layer can cause the heating element or burner to overheat locally, which accelerates component failure and, in gas units, can create the rumbling or popping sound many homeowners notice as sediment shifts during heating cycles.

How Often to Flush

Annually is the standard recommendation for most households on municipal water. Homes with harder water, or those without a functioning water softener, may benefit from flushing every 6-9 months instead. A tank that's never been flushed since installation, sometimes years earlier, often needs a longer, more thorough initial flush to clear out an accumulated layer before returning to a normal annual schedule.

What Happens If You Never Flush

A tank that's never been maintained will still function, often for years, but it will typically fail earlier than a well-maintained one, run less efficiently the whole time, and show early symptoms like rumbling sounds and reduced hot water volume well before outright failure. If your water heater is already showing these signs, see our water heater repair page for how we diagnose whether it's a fixable sediment issue or a sign the tank itself needs replacement.

If your household deals with noticeably hard water, pairing regular flushing with a water softener addresses the root cause rather than just managing the symptom every year.

How to Flush a Water Heater Yourself

For homeowners comfortable doing basic maintenance, flushing a tank is a straightforward process: turn off power or gas to the unit, connect a garden hose to the drain valve near the tank's base, and drain the tank fully into a floor drain or outside. Turning on a hot water tap elsewhere in the house helps break the vacuum and speeds draining. Once fully drained, briefly opening the cold water supply flushes out remaining sediment before refilling the tank completely.

If you're not comfortable with this process, or if the tank hasn't been flushed in several years and you're unsure what condition the drain valve itself is in, since valves on old, unmaintained tanks sometimes fail or clog, it's worth having a professional handle the first flush at minimum.

Signs Your Tank Needs More Than a Routine Flush

If a tank has gone years without maintenance, a routine flush sometimes isn't enough to clear an accumulated hardened layer of sediment at the bottom. In these cases, the buildup can be thick enough that standard draining barely disturbs it, and the tank continues showing symptoms even after a flush attempt. This is a sign the tank may be closer to the end of its service life than its calendar age would suggest, since heavy sediment accumulation accelerates internal corrosion at the tank's lowest point.

A professional assessment at that stage can determine whether a more thorough flush resolves the issue or whether the tank's remaining lifespan is short enough that replacement makes more financial sense than continuing to maintain an aging unit.

When to Call a Professional Instead

If your water heater is still under manufacturer warranty, check whether DIY maintenance voids that coverage before draining it yourself. Some manufacturers require documented professional service to keep a warranty valid, which is worth confirming with your specific unit's paperwork before deciding to handle it on your own.

A professional visit also gives you a chance to have the anode rod checked at the same time, since that component degrades on its own schedule separate from sediment buildup and is easy to overlook if you're only focused on flushing.

Quick Answers

Does flushing void my water heater warranty? No, in most cases, flushing is required maintenance under most manufacturer warranties, not something that voids coverage.

Can I flush a tankless system the same way? No, tankless units require a different descaling process using a circulation pump, not a simple drain-and-refill.

Will flushing fix low hot water pressure? Sometimes, if sediment buildup is restricting flow. If pressure stays low after flushing, the cause is likely elsewhere in the system.

The Bottom Line

Flushing a water heater is inexpensive maintenance that meaningfully extends a tank's life and keeps it running efficiently. Skipping it for years doesn't cause immediate failure, but it does shorten the tank's usable life and raise energy costs the whole time. A five-minute check once a year is a small trade for avoiding an early, avoidable replacement. If you're not sure when your tank was last flushed, or ever was, treat this year as the year to find out and start the habit. Mark it on a calendar so next year is not another guessing game. Most homeowners find it easier to remember when tied to another seasonal task, like changing furnace filters or testing smoke detectors. It only takes a few minutes once it's part of the routine.
