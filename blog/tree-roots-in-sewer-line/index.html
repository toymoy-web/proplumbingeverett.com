Tree Roots in Your Sewer Line: Signs, Causes, and Real Fixes

If your sewer line keeps backing up at the same spot no matter how many times it's cleared, roots at a joint are the most likely explanation.

By the Everett Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-19

Tree roots don't randomly find their way into a sewer line. They grow toward moisture, and a sewer lateral leaking even a small amount of water at a joint is exactly the kind of moisture source roots are built to seek out. Once a root finds its way into a crack or gap at a pipe joint, it doesn't stop growing, and over time it can fully block the line or widen the original crack into a structural problem.

Why This Is Especially Common in Clay Pipe

Clay sewer lateral pipe, standard in homes built before roughly 1970, has a joint every few feet, and each joint is a potential weak point. Modern PVC sewer pipe uses fewer, better-sealed joints and is far more resistant to this kind of intrusion. That's the specific reason older neighborhoods, like much of Everett's pre-1970s housing stock and Snohomish's even older First Street Historic District, see this problem so much more consistently than newer construction.

Signs You're Dealing With Root Intrusion

A few patterns point specifically toward roots rather than a simple clog:

  • The same drain or the whole main line backs up repeatedly, even after being professionally cleared.
  • Gurgling from toilets when a washing machine or dishwasher drains.
  • An unusually green or lush patch of lawn directly above where the sewer line runs.
  • Slow drains throughout the house rather than just one fixture.
  • A camera inspection showing visible roots at one or more pipe joints.

Why Cutting the Roots Back Doesn't Solve It Permanently

Standard drain snaking with a cutting head clears roots temporarily, but it doesn't repair the crack or gap that let the roots grow in during the first place. Roots regrow at the same weak point, often within months, which is why a line with a genuine root intrusion history keeps coming back even after repeated cleanings.

What Actually Stops It

Hydro jetting clears root growth more thoroughly than standard snaking, since the high-pressure water scours the full pipe interior rather than just cutting a channel through the current blockage. But for a joint with confirmed, recurring root intrusion, the only permanent fix addresses the crack itself, either through trenchless spot repair or lining at that specific joint, or full replacement if the damage is more widespread.

See our sewer line repair page for how we diagnose and fix root intrusion at the source, rather than treating the symptom every few months.

Can You Prevent Root Intrusion in the First Place?

If you're planning new landscaping near a known sewer line path, choosing tree species with less aggressive root systems, and planting them further from the line, reduces future risk. For an existing mature tree already near the line, removal isn't always necessary or practical, but knowing the tree's location relative to your sewer lateral is useful information to have on hand if problems develop later.

For homes with clay pipe that hasn't yet shown symptoms, some homeowners choose a preventive camera inspection to check joint condition before any backup happens, particularly if a known large tree sits directly above or near the line's path.

What a Camera Inspection Actually Shows

A sewer camera inspection isn't just a formality before quoting a repair. It shows the exact location of root intrusion, whether it's occurring at one joint or several, and whether the surrounding pipe shows other signs of deterioration like cracking or a belly. This level of detail is what separates a targeted, cost-effective repair from an oversized project that replaces more pipe than actually needs it.

Root Intrusion vs. Other Causes of Backups

Not every recurring backup is roots. Grease buildup, a collapsed section unrelated to roots, or a genuine capacity issue on an undersized older line can all produce similar symptoms. This is exactly why a camera inspection matters more than guessing based on symptoms alone: treating a grease problem as if it were root intrusion, or the reverse, means paying for a fix that doesn't address the actual cause.

If your line has never been inspected and you're dealing with unexplained recurring issues, starting with the camera inspection itself, before committing to any specific repair method, is the responsible first step.

Quick Answers

Will roots come back after cutting? Often yes, at the same joint, unless the underlying crack is repaired, not just the roots removed.

Does homeowner's insurance cover root damage? Generally no, root intrusion is considered gradual damage from normal tree growth, not a sudden covered event.

How often should an older line be inspected? Every 2-3 years for a home with known clay pipe and mature trees nearby, or sooner if symptoms appear.

The Bottom Line

A backup that keeps returning at the same spot is telling you something specific: the crack that let roots in hasn't been repaired, just the roots themselves removed. A camera inspection identifies the real fix before you pay for another temporary one. Keep a copy of any camera inspection footage for your own records, since it's useful reference if the same issue resurfaces years later. A line that's been properly repaired at the joint level should not need the same repair again for many years, if the underlying crack was actually addressed rather than just cleared. Ask directly whether a quoted repair addresses the crack itself or only clears the current growth. That single question separates a permanent fix from a temporary one dressed up as permanent. It's a fair question to ask any contractor before agreeing to the work, and a straight answer is a reasonable thing to expect.
