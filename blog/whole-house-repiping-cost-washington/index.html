How Much Does Whole-House Repiping Cost in Washington State?

There's no single number that applies to every home. Here's what actually determines the cost of a repipe project.

By the Everett Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-19

Whole-house repiping cost varies more than most other plumbing projects because it depends on so many home-specific factors: square footage, number of fixtures, wall and flooring type, and which material you choose. Rather than quote a single misleading average, it's more useful to walk through what actually drives the number up or down for a specific home.

Copper vs. PEX: The Biggest Material Decision

Copper has a longer proven track record and better resistance to physical damage, at a meaningfully higher material cost. PEX is more affordable and, because it's flexible enough to snake through existing wall cavities, typically requires less demolition to install, which lowers the labor side of the project too. For most residential repiping in Washington State today, PEX has become the standard choice, though copper remains the right call for certain exposed runs or specific homeowner preferences.

Full vs. Partial Repiping

Not every home needs every pipe replaced. If part of a house was previously updated, maybe a kitchen remodel that replaced supply lines in that area, a partial repipe addressing only the remaining galvanized sections can cost significantly less than a full whole-home project. This only makes sense when the untouched sections are genuinely isolated from the rest of the system, which is something worth confirming during an assessment rather than assuming.

Access and Restoration

A repipe in a home with unfinished basement and crawlspace access is significantly less expensive than one where walls need to be opened and later patched, textured, and repainted. Finished spaces add real restoration cost on top of the plumbing work itself, and it's worth getting a clear breakdown of which costs are plumbing labor versus drywall and paint restoration.

Fixture Count and Home Size

More bathrooms and water-using appliances mean more connection points, which scales both material and labor. A larger home with multiple bathrooms will cost proportionally more than a smaller one, though the per-fixture cost often decreases slightly on larger projects due to efficiency of scale.

Permits

Everett and most Snohomish County jurisdictions require permitting for whole-home repiping projects, and this is a real cost factor worth building into the total budget rather than treating as an afterthought.

For homes in Mukilteo's Old Town specifically, where original galvanized lines dating to before 1950 remain common, we frequently see full repiping recommended over continued spot repairs once a homeowner has had two or more unrelated pipe failures. See our repiping page for the full repair-vs-repipe comparison, and our water line repair & replacement page if the issue is the buried service line rather than the interior plumbing.

How Contractors Typically Structure a Repiping Quote

A thorough repiping quote should separate the cost into distinct categories: material cost for the pipe itself, labor for installation, and restoration for any walls, flooring, or finishes disturbed during the work. A quote that bundles everything into one number makes it harder to understand what you're actually paying for, and harder to compare against a second opinion.

It's reasonable to ask a contractor to walk through each of these categories separately, and to explain which parts of the home will need walls opened versus which sections can be reached through existing basement or crawlspace access. This transparency is what lets you compare quotes meaningfully rather than just comparing bottom-line numbers.

Financing and Timing Considerations

Whole-house repiping is a significant expense, and many homeowners choose to finance it rather than pay entirely upfront, particularly when the project is being done proactively rather than in response to an emergency failure. If you're already planning a kitchen or bathroom remodel, coordinating the repiping with that work can reduce total restoration costs, since walls that are already being opened for the remodel don't need to be opened a second time for the plumbing.

Getting an Accurate Quote

A quote given over the phone without seeing the home is, at best, a rough estimate. Accurate repiping pricing requires an in-person assessment of accessible pipe, fixture count, wall and flooring types, and any known problem areas. Be cautious of a quote that comes in dramatically lower than others for a comparable scope, since it may not account for restoration costs that surface once the walls are actually opened.

Quick Answers

Does homeowner's insurance cover repiping? No, repiping due to age or material degradation is considered a maintenance expense, not a covered peril.

Will I have water during the project? Most repiping projects require shutting off water for portions of each day, though a contractor can usually restore service by evening.

Does repiping increase home value? Yes, generally, since it removes a known liability that shows up in home inspections and can affect resale negotiations on older homes.

The Bottom Line

Repiping cost depends on your specific home more than any general average can capture. An in-person assessment that breaks out material, labor, and restoration costs separately is the only way to get a number you can actually compare against a second opinion. Get at least two quotes for a project this size, and ask each contractor the same specific questions about scope. A wide spread between quotes for the same described scope is a sign one of them is missing something, not necessarily that one is a better deal. Ask each bidder to walk the accessible pipe with you before finalizing anything, and ask what specifically triggers a change order once the walls are actually opened, so an unexpected finding doesn't turn into an unexpected bill. Getting this in writing before work starts protects both sides, no matter how much you trust the contractor going in. A written scope beats a verbal promise every time.
