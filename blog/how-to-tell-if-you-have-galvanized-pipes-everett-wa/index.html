Does Your Everett Home Have Galvanized Pipes? How to Check

If your home is older, there's a real chance you're still running galvanized steel supply lines. Here's how to check without hiring anyone.

By the Everett Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026-10-12

Galvanized steel pipe was widely used for residential water supply lines in older US homes, and some Everett houses from that era may still have original galvanized plumbing in place. The only way to know is to check, which is what the tests below are for. The problem isn't that galvanized pipe was poorly made. It's that the zinc coating that gives it its name eventually wears through, exposing the steel underneath to decades of corrosion from the inside out.

Knowing whether you have galvanized pipe matters because it changes how you think about maintenance, repair, and eventually replacement. A pinhole leak in a 60-year-old galvanized line isn't a random bad break. It's a predictable failure of a material that's simply past its service life, and it's often a sign the rest of the system is close behind.

The Two-Minute Test

You don't need a plumber to do this first check. Find an exposed section of your water supply line, typically in a basement, crawlspace, or utility closet, and look at its color. Galvanized pipe is dull gray, sometimes with a slightly silvery sheen when newer, darkening toward black as it corrodes. Copper looks like a penny, reddish-orange when clean. PEX is usually white, red, or blue plastic tubing, and won't be confused with metal pipe at all.

If the color test isn't conclusive, because the pipe has been painted or is heavily corroded, grab a strong magnet. Galvanized steel is magnetic. A magnet will stick firmly to it. Copper and PEX are not magnetic at all, and a magnet will simply fall off. This single test resolves almost every ambiguous case.

Signs of Trouble Even If You Can't See the Pipe

Not every home lets you easily access exposed pipe, especially if your supply lines run entirely through finished walls and slab. In that case, look for secondary signs instead:

  • Reduced water pressure throughout the house, especially compared to what you remember from years ago.
  • Rust-colored or discolored water, particularly noticeable first thing in the morning before any water has run.
  • A metallic taste to your tap water.
  • Uneven pressure between fixtures, since corrosion builds up unevenly along a galvanized system.
  • An older home with no documented record of a prior repiping project.

What to Do If You Confirm Galvanized Pipe

Finding galvanized pipe doesn't mean you need to repipe your entire house tomorrow. A galvanized system that's still functioning without leaks or major pressure loss can continue in service, though it's worth having a professional assess overall condition, particularly if you're planning any renovation that would open up walls anyway.

If you're already seeing symptoms, reduced pressure, discolored water, or a recent leak, that's a stronger signal the system is failing broadly, not just at one point. Why Galvanized Pipe Was Ever Used in the First Place

It's worth understanding why an entire generation of homes was built with a material that we now know fails predictably. Before galvanized steel came into wide use, other materials were used for water supply lines. Galvanized steel, coated in zinc to resist rust, was a genuine improvement at the time, and it performed reasonably well for several decades before the coating's protective effect wore through.

The issue isn't that builders made a mistake. It's that no water supply material lasts forever, and galvanized steel's real service life, typically 40-50 years, has run out for many older homes. Copper, introduced more widely from the 1960s onward, and PEX plastic tubing, standard in new construction since the 1990s, both have longer expected lifespans and don't share galvanized steel's internal corrosion problem.

What a Professional Assessment Actually Checks

If you've confirmed galvanized pipe and want a professional opinion before deciding on next steps, a proper assessment goes beyond just identifying the material. We check water pressure at multiple fixtures throughout the house to gauge how evenly the system has corroded, inspect any accessible sections for visible pitting or thinning. Ask about your history of leaks or repairs, since a pattern of unrelated failures in different rooms is itself diagnostic information.

This full picture is what actually determines whether a targeted repair, a partial repipe of the worst sections, or a full whole-home replacement makes the most financial sense for your specific situation, rather than defaulting to the most expensive option out of caution.

One Test Doesn't Tell the Whole Story

Finding galvanized pipe in one accessible spot, like a basement supply line, doesn't guarantee the whole system matches. Partial updates happen, especially if a prior owner replaced a section during a remodel. Test more than one location if you can access multiple spots, since a mixed system changes the picture of what actually needs attention.

What the City of Everett Says About Service Lines

The City of Everett's lead service line inventory page says most service lines in the City system are copper or plastic, that all were determined to be non-lead for the inventory, and that iron-based lines, including galvanized, are less common. That inventory is about the service line that brings water to the property, not the pipes inside your house.

So the magnet and scratch tests above are still how you check your own plumbing. If you are in Mukilteo or Edmonds, the same tests apply. If you confirm galvanized, see our repiping page.

Quick Answers

Can I tell if a pipe is galvanized with a magnet?

Yes. A magnet sticks to galvanized steel but not to copper or plastic, and a light scratch that shows silver-gray metal also points to galvanized. Test an exposed section of pipe rather than a fitting.

Does Everett's lead service line inventory tell me if my house has galvanized pipe?

No. The City's inventory covers the service line that brings water to the property, and it says iron-based lines, including galvanized, are less common. It does not tell you what is inside your walls, so run the magnet and scratch tests on your own exposed pipe.

The Bottom Line

A five-minute magnet test tells you more than guessing based on your home's age alone. If you confirm galvanized pipe, that's useful information whether or not you act on it immediately, since it changes how you'll interpret future pressure drops, discolored water, or an unexpected leak. A repiped home also tends to hold its value better at resale, since a known galvanized system is a common inspection flag for buyers.
