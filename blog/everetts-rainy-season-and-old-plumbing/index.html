Everett's Rainy Season and What It Does to Older Plumbing

It's not just that everything gets wetter. Rising groundwater changes pressure on your foundation and pipes in a specific, predictable way.

By the Everett Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026-10-12

Everett's rainy season runs roughly October through April, and homeowners often notice a consistent pattern every year: plumbing problems that seemed dormant all summer suddenly show up as soon as the rain starts. This isn't coincidence. There's a specific mechanism connecting rainfall to plumbing failure, and understanding it helps explain why the fix isn't always what it first appears to be.

How Rain Actually Reaches Your Pipes

Rainfall doesn't directly damage indoor plumbing. What it does is raise the water table, the level below which soil is saturated, across the whole region. As that water table rises closer to the surface, it increases hydrostatic pressure against anything buried or built into the ground: foundations, basement walls, and buried water and sewer lines alike.

Why This Hits Older Homes Harder

A foundation built to modern drainage standards is designed to handle this seasonal pressure change. A foundation relying on original perimeter drain tile that's now decades past its functional life isn't. That's the connection between a home's age and seasonal basement flooding: older homes aren't inherently worse, but their drainage systems were never designed for 50-plus years of continuous use without replacement.

What This Means for Supply and Sewer Lines

The same water table rise affects buried pipes directly. A sewer lateral with a small crack or root intrusion at a joint sees more water infiltration during high-water-table periods, which is one reason sewer problems can show up in the wet months. Water supply lines see less direct effect from groundwater, but soil movement during wet-to-dry seasonal cycles adds mechanical stress that can accelerate failure in already-corroded galvanized pipe.

What Actually Helps

Since the root cause is groundwater pressure against inadequate drainage, the fix targets that pressure directly rather than just treating each symptom as it appears. Basement waterproofing with a properly sized interior drain system and sump pump addresses the pressure at the foundation. For sewer lines, resolving root intrusion or structural damage at the joint level, rather than repeatedly clearing the same backup, addresses it there.

Doing this work before the rainy season starts, rather than during an active flood, gives far more flexibility in scheduling and often costs less than emergency response once water is already inside.

The Bottom Line

Everett's rainy season doesn't cause plumbing problems directly. It exposes drainage and pipe weaknesses that were already there, on a predictable annual schedule. Addressing the underlying weakness once, rather than the symptom every winter, is what actually breaks the cycle.

Why Some Years Are Worse Than Others

Not every winter produces the same call volume, even with the same aging infrastructure citywide. A wetter-than-average season, or several consecutive years of above-normal rainfall, raises the baseline water table higher than a typical year, which is why some winters feel like everyone's basement is flooding at once while others pass relatively quietly. This year-to-year variation doesn't change the underlying cause, just how visibly it shows up.

A Practical Way to Think About Timing

If you're planning any drainage or plumbing work on an older Everett home, late summer and early fall, before the rainy season starts, is the ideal window. Contractors have more scheduling flexibility, the ground is drier for any excavation work, and you're addressing the problem before the seasonal pressure that actually reveals it. Waiting until the first flood of the season to start looking for help means competing with every other homeowner in the same situation for the same limited emergency capacity.

This same logic applies to repiping decisions too: a system that's been narrowly holding on through several rainy seasons is a better candidate for proactive replacement before the next one than for another round of reactive repairs.

What Changes Between Seasons

Basement and sump pump problems tend to track the water table through the wet months far more closely than any single storm event. Sewer problems can follow a similar, slightly delayed pattern, since root-affected joints take a bit longer to saturate and fail than open foundation cracks do.

This predictability is actually useful. It means the window for proactive work, roughly May through September, is well-defined and worth planning around rather than treating plumbing maintenance as something to think about only when a problem is already active. It also means a homeowner who's had one bad winter has real, useful information for planning the next one, rather than treating each flood as an isolated surprise.

Everett's Rain Pattern in Numbers

NOAA's 1991-2020 climate normals for the Everett Snohomish County Airport station show about 33 inches of precipitation a year. Roughly three quarters of it, about 26 inches, falls from October through April. July averages under an inch, while November averages just under 5 inches.

That spread is why the dry months are the right time for sump pump testing and drainage work. If you live near the water, see our Lake Stevens and Mill Creek pages, and our sump pump page for pump service.

Sources: NOAA 1991-2020 climate normals, Everett Snohomish County Airport station (USW00024222).

Quick Answers

Does rain actually get into the pipes?

No, the connection is through rising groundwater pressure against foundations and buried pipe, not water entering the plumbing system itself.

Does every old home in Everett have this problem?

Not necessarily, homes with prior drainage upgrades or repiping are much less affected.

Is this unique to Everett?

No, but the combination of marine-climate rainfall and aging drainage makes it a real pattern here, and every marine-climate city in the region deals with some version of it. The specific combination of factors matters more than the general climate description alone, which is why a citywide fix rarely applies evenly to every neighborhood, and why a site-specific assessment beats a generic seasonal checklist alone. Two homes a mile apart can have genuinely different risk profiles based on soil type and construction era alone, which is exactly why we ask about your specific address rather than quoting a blanket answer.

How much of Everett's yearly rain falls between October and April?

NOAA's 1991-2020 normals for the Everett Snohomish County Airport station put annual precipitation at about 33.4 inches. October through April accounts for about 25.9 inches of it, or roughly 77 percent.
