Plumbing to Check Before Buying an Older Everett Home

A standard home inspection often misses the specific pipe-material and sewer-lateral questions that matter most in an older home.

By the Everett Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026-10-12

A general home inspection checks that plumbing fixtures work and looks for obvious leaks, but it doesn't always dig into pipe material, sewer lateral condition, or the specific age-related risks that matter most in an older home. Knowing what to ask before closing can save you from an expensive surprise in the first year of ownership.

Ask What the Supply Lines Are Made Of

A general inspector may note visible pipe material but not always flag the implications clearly. Ask specifically whether the home has galvanized steel supply lines, and if so, whether any portion has already been updated. Galvanized pipe past 40-50 years old is a real known liability, not a cosmetic detail, and it's worth factoring into your offer or negotiation, not discovering after you've moved in.

Ask About the Sewer Lateral Specifically

Standard inspections often don't include a sewer camera inspection unless specifically requested. For an older home, especially one with mature trees on the property, requesting a sewer camera inspection before closing is worth the modest additional cost. Root intrusion and pipe deterioration in an aging sewer lateral are expensive to discover after you own the home, so they are worth checking before closing.

Check the Water Heater's Real Age

A water heater's manufacture date is usually printed on a sticker on the unit itself, not always obvious from a casual look. If it's near or past the typical 10-12 year service life, budget for replacement soon after moving in rather than assuming it has years left simply because it's currently working.

Ask About Any Prior Basement or Crawlspace Flooding

Sellers are required to disclose known material defects, but flooding history sometimes gets described vaguely. Ask directly whether the basement or crawlspace has flooded, when, and what if anything was done about it. A home with undisclosed or unaddressed flooding history can mean a real, ongoing drainage problem waiting for the next rainy season.

Getting a Plumbing-Specific Assessment Before Closing

Beyond the standard home inspection, a dedicated plumbing assessment covering pipe material, water pressure at multiple fixtures, and water heater condition gives you real leverage during negotiation and a clear picture of what to budget for after closing. See our repiping page for what a full-system assessment covers if you're already anticipating that need.

The Bottom Line

A standard inspection is a starting point, not the full picture, for an older home's plumbing. Asking these specific questions before closing, and considering a dedicated plumbing assessment for a genuinely old property, turns unknown risk into a known, negotiable cost.

Budgeting for What You Find

If a pre-purchase assessment confirms galvanized supply lines or sewer lateral issues, that's not necessarily a reason to walk away from an otherwise good house. It is a reason to factor a realistic repair or replacement budget into your offer, or to negotiate a credit at closing that reflects the real cost of addressing it in the first year or two of ownership rather than being surprised by it later.

Questions Worth Asking the Current Owner Directly

Beyond what a report shows, a direct conversation with the seller can surface useful context: have they had any plumbing repairs during their ownership, do they know the original construction date versus any addition or remodel dates, and has the sewer line ever backed up. Sellers aren't always required to volunteer this detail unprompted, but a direct question often gets a direct answer.

Working This Into Your Inspection Timeline

A sewer camera inspection and a plumbing-specific assessment both take a few hours to schedule and complete, which fits comfortably within most standard inspection contingency periods. Requesting them alongside your general home inspection, rather than as a separate follow-up after the fact, keeps your timeline intact while still getting the specific information a general inspection doesn't always surface.

This Applies Beyond Everett Proper

The same questions matter for older homes throughout the surrounding communities we serve. The same pre-purchase approach applies to older homes in Mukilteo, Edmonds, Snohomish and the rest of the area we serve. A buyer's agent familiar with the local housing stock can often flag which specific neighborhoods warrant this extra diligence before you even make an offer, since not every part of even one older neighborhood carries identical risk.

Buying in Everett: What the Public Records Say

Washington's seller disclosure form under RCW 64.06.020 includes questions about the plumbing system and the sewer connection. Read those answers closely and ask follow-up questions in writing about anything marked unknown.

In Everett, the City maintains sewer lines in the public right of way, and private owners are responsible for the side sewer that serves their property. Everett Municipal Code chapter 14.08 requires the abutting owner to repair and maintain it. That makes the pipe from the house to the connection a cost you would own after closing, so a camera inspection is a fair request. See our sewer line repair page, and if you are also looking at homes in Mukilteo or Snohomish, the same questions apply there.

Sources: RCW 64.06.020, City of Everett sewer page (everettwa.gov/588/Sewer), Everett Municipal Code chapter 14.08.

Quick Answers

Should I ask for a repipe credit or have the seller fix it?

A credit at closing usually gives you more control over the contractor and timeline than relying on the seller to arrange repairs before you take ownership.

Does title insurance cover plumbing issues?

No, title insurance covers ownership disputes, not physical defects like aging pipe.

Is a sewer camera inspection worth it on a newer home too?

Less critical, but still reasonable for any home over 25-30 years old with mature trees on the property.

Can I negotiate repairs instead of a price reduction?

Yes, either approach is common, though a credit typically gives the buyer more control over quality and timeline. Whichever route you choose, get the agreed scope in writing as part of the purchase contract, including which specific repairs or credits are tied to which findings. A vague verbal agreement offers little protection once the sale closes.

Should I still buy the home if it needs a full repipe?

Many buyers do, once the cost is known and reflected fairly in the purchase price rather than discovered as a surprise.

Who pays to repair the side sewer on a home in Everett?

Under Everett Municipal Code chapter 14.08, the abutting property owner must repair and maintain the side sewer, so after closing that is generally the buyer's responsibility. Confirm the details for a specific property with the City before you close.
